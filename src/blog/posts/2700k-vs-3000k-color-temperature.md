You don't need to know what 2700K means to choose great lighting — that's our job. But it helps to understand why the same fixture can look warm and inviting in one house and flat in another.

## What the "K" means

Color temperature is measured in kelvin (K). Lower numbers look warmer and more golden; higher numbers look whiter and cooler.

| Color temperature | How it looks | Where people like it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2700K** | Warm, golden, like a classic incandescent bulb | Living rooms, bedrooms, dining rooms |
| **3000K** | Soft white — still warm, a little crisper | Kitchens, baths, whole-house choice |
| **3500K–4000K** | Neutral to cool white | Garages, workshops, offices |
| **5000K+** | Bluish daylight | Utility spaces; rarely right in a home |

## 2700K or 3000K?

Both are good choices for a home. **2700K** feels cozier and flatters wood, brass and warm paint colors. **3000K** reads a little cleaner, which many people like with white cabinets, marble and cooler grays.

The bigger rule: **pick one and use it throughout the house.** When the kitchen is 3000K and the family room it opens into is 2700K, both rooms look slightly wrong. Mixed bulbs are the fastest way to make good fixtures look cheap.

## Two more numbers worth knowing

- **CRI (color rendering index):** how accurately the light shows colors. Look for **90 or higher** so skin tones, food and paint look right.
- **Dim-to-warm:** some LED fixtures and bulbs get warmer as you dim them, the way old incandescent bulbs did. Lovely in dining rooms and bedrooms.

## A note on recessed lights

Your can lights and your decorative fixtures should match. Recessed lights often get chosen by the electrician before anyone thinks about the chandelier, so ask what color temperature they're planning early — before they're installed.
